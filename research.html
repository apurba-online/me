Current Research

From Reconstructing CT Images to Understanding Patients

My current research began with a practical question: if the same patient can look different because of how a CT image is reconstructed, how much can we trust the quantitative information that comes afterward? That question led me first to generative CT reconstruction, then to region-adaptive reconstruction with reinforcement learning, and now to representation learning and patient-level prediction. RA-CMF sits at the center of that progression.

Chapter 01 / The Problem

The same anatomy can produce different quantitative CT measurements

The starting point of my PhD research is a problem that appears before classification, survival modeling, or radiomics-based prediction even begins: technical variation in CT can change the image without changing the patient.

Reconstruction kernels, acquisition settings, scanner characteristics, slice thickness, and noise behavior can all influence texture and image appearance. That matters because many downstream AI and radiomics methods assume that the measured image features reflect biology. If a substantial part of that signal is actually coming from reconstruction choices, the downstream model may learn technical variation instead of disease-related information.

I therefore started by asking a more basic question: can I reconstruct a CT image from one reconstruction domain into another target domain while preserving the patient's anatomy, subtle lung texture, lesion appearance, and quantitative image features?

That question became the first stage of my research: build a generative reconstruction model that learns the transformation between paired CT reconstructions of the same anatomy.

Chapter 02 / First Step

I began with Conditional MeanFlow as the global reconstruction model

Instead of treating reconstruction as a single direct image-to-image mapping, I use Conditional MeanFlow to model how an intermediate image state should move toward the target reconstruction.

The source CT remains part of the conditioning throughout the transformation. This is important because the goal is not to generate a plausible-looking CT image from scratch. The goal is to transform the reconstruction characteristics while keeping the anatomy tied to the observed patient image.

In the model, the current image state, the source CT, and time/reference-time information are processed through a 2D U-Net. The network predicts an image-sized velocity field that describes how the current state should evolve along the reconstruction trajectory.

Chapter 03 / What Was Missing

A strong global reconstruction still does not mean every region is equally well reconstructed

Conditional MeanFlow gave me a strong global reconstruction, but the next question came directly from looking at where the remaining errors were located.

Reconstruction difficulty is not spatially uniform. Large homogeneous regions may already be reconstructed very well, while smaller areas containing complex pulmonary structure, lesion boundaries, tumor-adjacent tissue, or subtle texture may remain more difficult. Applying the same amount of additional processing everywhere is inefficient, and it can disturb regions that were already correct.

That changed the problem. Instead of asking only how do I reconstruct the image?, I started asking where is more reconstruction actually needed, how much should be done there, and when should the model stop?

This is where RA-CMF emerged: keep the strong global MeanFlow reconstruction, then learn how to selectively spend extra computation only where it is useful.

Chapter 04 / RA-CMF

RA-CMF turns local reconstruction into a sequential decision problem

RA-CMF: Region-Adaptive Conditional MeanFlow combines the global Conditional MeanFlow backbone with a reinforcement-learning controller for spatially selective refinement.

After the global reconstruction, the controller observes information from the current reconstruction process and decides which regions still justify additional refinement. It can allocate different local micro-step budgets to different regions, control the total refinement budget across the slice, and stop when additional updates are no longer useful.

I use an actor-critic PPO formulation because these decisions are connected over time. A region that needs refinement at one step may not need another step later. The controller therefore learns not only where to refine, but also how much and when to stop.

My emphasis is subtle texture rather than edge enhancement. The desired behavior is a small, stable correction that moves difficult regions toward the target while preserving anatomy and avoiding artificial image content.

Chapter 05 / How I Study It

The reconstruction task is built around paired CT images of the same anatomy

To make the reconstruction problem meaningful, the model needs to see how the same anatomy appears under different reconstruction settings. In the main RA-CMF study, GE Standard is treated as the source reconstruction and GE Lung as the target reconstruction.

Pairing is performed within the same patient and imaging session, with matched spatial position and supported slice thicknesses of 1.25 mm or 2.5 mm. I use a CT window of −800 to 600 HU during the main training and evaluation pipeline.

The model operates on individual 2D slices, but the data split is performed strictly at the patient level. That distinction matters because neighboring CT slices are highly correlated. Slices from one patient are never shared across training, validation, and testing.

Why patient separation matters

Approximately 19,500 paired slices provide many reconstruction examples, but neighboring slices are correlated. The patient-level split prevents leakage across data partitions.

Why tumor ROIs matter

Whole-image metrics can hide local errors. Tumor-region evaluation tests whether the reconstruction remains reliable where clinically important structures are located.

Chapter 06 / What I Found

The regional stage improves both image fidelity and quantitative consistency

I evaluate the reconstruction at several levels because a visually good CT image is not automatically a quantitatively reliable CT image.

Full-image PSNR and SSIM measure overall reconstruction fidelity. Tumor-region PSNR and SSIM examine local quality. Radiomic concordance correlation coefficient measures how consistently quantitative features are recovered across first-order and texture feature families. Noise power spectrum analysis is used to study whether the reconstructed noise and texture behavior moves toward the target domain.

In the current evaluation, RA-CMF improves over the comparison methods in tumor-region quality and full-image reconstruction while maintaining strong radiomic consistency.

The result changed the next question for me. Once the reconstruction model had learned where the image is difficult and how the image changes along its trajectory, I wanted to know whether that internal information could become a useful representation of the CT itself.

Chapter 07 / What Came Next

I am now turning the reconstruction process into a CT representation

My current extension of RA-CMF asks whether the reconstruction trajectory contains information that is useful beyond reconstruction itself.

I keep the trained RA-CMF reconstruction backbone and controller frozen, then learn a separate representation head from internal MeanFlow features. The representation combines global information from the slice with local information from regions that the controller considers important.

A CLS-like learnable aggregation mechanism combines one global token with selected regional tokens. The current implementation produces a 512-dimensional late-trajectory slice embedding. A VICReg-style objective aligns related views of the same anatomy across intermediate reconstruction states and the paired target while preventing representational collapse.

Global context

The representation keeps whole-slice information instead of reducing the image to only a collection of local patches.

Controller-selected regions

Local tokens come from spatial areas that the RA-CMF controller identifies as informative or difficult during reconstruction.

Trajectory information

Intermediate and late reconstruction states provide related views of the same underlying anatomy.

Compact embedding

The final vector is intended to become a transferable CT fingerprint for downstream modeling rather than another reconstructed image.

Chapter 08 / From Images to Patients

The next step is to move from slice embeddings to patient-level prediction

A 2D slice is useful for learning reconstruction behavior, but a patient-level clinical question cannot end at a single slice. My next stage is therefore about aggregation and multimodal prediction.

I am studying how multiple slice embeddings can be combined into a representation of the complete CT study and then fused with clinical context. This connects the reconstruction work to downstream outcome and risk modeling, including treatment-free-survival analysis.

A parallel direction focuses on early detection using low-dose CT. The goal is to test whether reconstruction-aware imaging representations can help identify lung-cancer-related events earlier, including a 12-month predictive endpoint.

The overall progression is intentional: first reduce technical imaging variation, then learn a representation from that controlled imaging process, then ask the patient-level predictive question.

Chapter 09 / Where It Is Going

Generalization, pathology preservation, and clinical usefulness are the next tests

The current work establishes the reconstruction framework and the path toward learned CT representations. The broader research program now needs to answer whether these gains remain stable across larger cohorts, different acquisition conditions, scanner vendors, and downstream tasks.

I am extending the work through additional CT cohorts, reproducibility datasets, phantom data, and downstream predictive experiments. An equally important question is safety: because generative reconstruction can alter image content, I am explicitly interested in whether subtle pathological findings remain unchanged while technical reconstruction variability is reduced.

External cohorts

LIDC-IDRI and NSCLC Radiogenomics provide additional settings for representation, prediction, and generalization studies.

Repeatability

RIDER and related paired/repeat imaging resources provide a way to test whether reconstruction improves quantitative reproducibility.

Scanner variation

GE and Siemens phantom data are part of the broader strategy for controlled analysis of protocol- and vendor-related variation.

Downstream value

Classification, risk, survival, and early-detection experiments will determine whether better reconstruction and representation actually improve predictive modeling.

The research started with a reconstruction problem, but the larger goal is not reconstruction by itself. It is to build imaging representations that are less sensitive to technical variation and more useful for understanding what is happening with the patient.
